What is an FDE?

1.1Definition

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is the engineer who makes a software company’s product work in the customer’s real environment. They get access to the customer’s data and systems, write the code that goes to production, measure the outcome against the customer’s business metric and carry what they learn in the field back into the product.

The name comes from military terminology. A forward deployed unit is one stationed ahead, at the front line.

Writes production code

The integration, workflow and evaluation code they write runs in the customer’s production environment. Monitoring, maintenance and incident response are part of the job too.

Works alongside the customer

They watch users do their work, personally chase data access and security approvals, and sit in the same meetings as the customer’s teams. Much of their time can be spent at the customer’s office.

Owns the outcome

Success is a business metric measured at the customer: processing time, error rate, cost. The metric is agreed in writing at the start of the project.

Changes the product

They take solutions that recur across customers to the product team. Code written for one customer becomes a product component that ships ready for the next.

1.2Origins: Palantir

The role emerged at Palantir in the early 2010s, where it was known internally as Delta (The Pragmatic Engineer). Palantir still describes itself on its careers page as built on three roles:

DevSoftware Engineer

Builds core products such as Gotham and Foundry. In Palantir’s words: one capability, many customers.

DeltaForward Deployed Software Engineer

Deploys to a single customer and makes the product work on that customer’s data and problem. In Palantir’s words: one customer, many capabilities.

EchoDeployment Strategist

Owns the customer’s business problem, the stakeholders and product adoption.

The distinction between “one capability, many customers” and “one customer, many capabilities” comes from Palantir’s Dev versus Delta post. According to a former Palantir engineer’s essay, FDEs spent 3 to 4 days a week at the customer’s office, and product engineers turned what they built in the field into product. The first version of Foundry was largely shaped this way. According to The Pragmatic Engineer, Palantir had more FDEs than “regular” software engineers until around 2016.

The same essay describes the model as a rare case of a services company turning into a product company, and puts numbers on it: Palantir’s 2023 gross margin was 80%, against Accenture’s 32% the same year.

1.3The AI surge

For an LLM-based product to create value at an enterprise customer, four kinds of work are needed, and all four are specific to that customer:

  • Integration. Giving the model access to data in the customer’s legacy systems: SOAP services, document archives, mainframes.
  • Evaluation. Answering “Does it work correctly?” with the customer’s data and labels from the customer’s own experts.
  • Security and compliance. Data residency, authorization, audit trail, KVKK (Türkiye’s personal data protection law) or GDPR.
  • Workflow. Designing whose screen the model output lands on, and how it feeds into which decision.

According to OpenAI’s FDE leadership, in roughly 80% of enterprise projects the problem is deployment (The Next Web). Job postings describe the work the same way. OpenAI’s posting defines the FDE as the engineer who leads end-to-end production deployments of frontier models with its most strategic customers. Anthropic’s lists delivering MCP servers, sub-agents and agent skills for use in the customer’s production workflows.

a16z’s June 2025 essay reads the model as a deliberate trade: AI startups accept the lower gross margin that FDEs bring as the price of embedding in the customer’s business systems. The same firm’s January 2026 essay warns that most companies copying the model risk turning into expensive services firms.

Example scenario: Insurance claim file review

Fictional customer12 weeksMotor insurance

2.1The problem

A mid-sized Turkish insurer has a claims handler check every file before it pays out on a kasko (motor own-damage insurance) claim. The handler opens the documents in the file one by one and cross-checks them: does the plate on the accident report match the vehicle registration, did the accident happen within the policy period, does the invoice amount add up. The FDE comes to this insurer for 12 weeks from a software company that sells a document processing and AI platform.

2.4Problems in the field

Two tasks for the same file

PROBLEM
Long-running jobs on large PDFs led the queue to hand the same message to a second worker; the handler saw two tasks for the same file.
FIX
A unique key per task (idempotency). The same job cannot open a second task.

Plate and ID number misreads

PROBLEM
On handwritten accident reports and poor scans, 0/O and 1/I get mixed up.
FIX
Plate format validation and a checksum on the TC kimlik no (Turkish national ID number). Any field that fails is flagged to the handler.

Monday morning slowdown

PROBLEM
The legacy policy service is capped at 10 requests per second. Paperwork that piled up over the weekend pushed the queue to 3,100 jobs.
FIX
One call per file, caching and rate limiting. The wait dropped from 41 minutes to 3.

Citations to clauses that are not in the policy

PROBLEM
While writing its rationale, the model invented clause numbers that do not exist in the policy.
FIX
The model may only quote verbatim from the clauses it is given; every quote is looked up in the policy and verified.

A model update broke dates

PROBLEM
A new model version returned some dates as month/day instead of day/month.
FIX
The model version was pinned. No change goes live without passing the test set.

Several documents in one PDF

PROBLEM
Insurance agents upload all the paperwork for a file as a single PDF.
FIX
Document type is detected page by page and the PDF is split automatically.
